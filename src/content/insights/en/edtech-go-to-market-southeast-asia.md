In Southeast Asian education, a great demo gets you roughly 15% of the way to revenue. The budget was set months before you arrived, the person who loves your product usually does not sign the cheque, and the contract goes to whoever understood the buying process rather than whoever had the better interface.

Here is the playbook I use with EdTech companies launching in the region: who buys, how to structure a pilot so it ends in a decision, how to price across very different purchasing powers, and what to do in the first 90 days.

## Who actually buys, market by market

The region is not one market, and treating it as one is the most expensive mistake available. Four differences change your plan:

- **Singapore.** Two segments: MOE-linked schools and clusters, where adoption often follows a top-down evaluation and the school year starts in January; and a large private and international school segment that buys faster, with fewer signatures. Decision windows are short and tied to the calendar year — miss the November-to-January window and you are often waiting twelve months. Sequence your launch around the school-year budget cycle, not your product roadmap.
- **Indonesia.** The school year starts in July, and the private school and tutoring market is enormous and price-sensitive. National-scale direct sales is not realistic for a small team; partner-led motion is how most companies get coverage. Budget for partner enablement and margin, and expect to need Indonesian-language proof rather than translated brochures.
- **Vietnam.** Fast-moving private chains and English-language and STEM programmes, hungry for evidence of learning outcomes. Buyers move quickly when the proof is credible and stall indefinitely when it is not. One strong local reference account beats three regional logos.
- **Malaysia and the Philippines.** Worth entering after one or two reference accounts, usually via the partners already selling into schools. Expansion, not launch.

The pattern underneath: in every market the champion is a teacher, a head of department or an academic lead, and the signature comes from a principal, a finance lead or a cluster director. Your marketing has to arm the champion with something they can forward upward — one page, one outcome, one number.

## The pilot trap

Pilots are how education buys. An unbounded pilot is also how deals quietly die: six months of enthusiasm, no decision, and a renewal conversation starting from zero.

A pilot should be a contract with a beginning, a number and an end:

- **A written pilot agreement**, even a two-page one, with success criteria named before it starts. Typical: completion rate above 80%, assessment pass rate above 85%, or a measurable reduction in teacher preparation time.
- **A named executive sponsor** on the buyer's side — not the champion, the person who can sign. If nobody will put their name on the pilot, that is your answer.
- **A fixed 8–12 week window.** Longer pilots rarely convert better; they convert worse, because attention dissipates.
- **The review meeting in the calendar before kick-off.** This is the highest-leverage practice in this article. A pilot without a scheduled review has no ending.
- **A conversion target you track honestly.** Pilot-to-paid conversion of 30–50% is good, 20–25% is the median, and under 15% means you scoped it wrong — wrong segment, wrong sponsor, or criteria the buyer never agreed to.

If you are running pilots at a 15% conversion rate, the problem is not your sales team. It is that pilots are being given away as a courtesy rather than sold as a decision.

## Pricing for purchasing power without wrecking your ACV

The mistake is pricing per seat in markets where per-student budgets are a fraction of Singapore's, then discounting when it does not land. The result is a low anchor you will never lift.

- **Price by segment, not by seat.** School-level deployments land at S$8,000–25,000 a year. School clusters and private networks — the segment worth structuring the whole motion around — run S$60,000–250,000. Higher-education institutions sit at S$40,000–150,000, and corporate L&D at S$15,000–60,000.
- **Bill in local currency where you can.** It removes a real procurement objection and stops the FX conversation becoming a discount conversation.
- **Make the entry tier deliberately small.** A low-friction first step is fine, provided the expansion path is documented and the next tier is obviously better value.
- **Make every discount buy something.** Term length, a case study with named results, three reference calls, a wider rollout, or payment upfront. A discount that buys nothing trains the buyer to ask for the next one.
- **Do not discount to match a local competitor's per-seat price.** You are not selling the same thing, and the deal will not fund the support it requires.

## The first 90 days of a launch

A sequence with weeks attached, assuming one marketer and one founder selling:

- **Weeks 1–3 — research, not production.** Ten to fifteen win/loss and buyer interviews. A competitive teardown in each target market. A written map of how budget actually gets approved in your priority segment, including who blocks it. No new website copy yet.
- **Weeks 3–5 — decisions.** Positioning, ICP, packaging and pricing agreed with your sales lead and written into one page. Stress-test the price with three real prospects, not with your team. This is the step almost everyone skips under launch pressure, and the one that determines whether the next quarter is expensive.
- **Weeks 5–8 — build the machinery.** The two channels you will actually run, the sales narrative and one-pager the champion can forward, objection handling for the three objections you hear most, and the pilot agreement template with its success criteria.
- **Weeks 8–12 — launch against leading indicators.** A weekly scorecard: qualified opportunities by source, pilot starts, pilot-to-paid rate, cycle length. Not registrations, not downloads. If you cannot see the leading indicators weekly, you will discover the launch failed a quarter after it did.

## Common mistakes

Six that between them explain most stalled EdTech launches in Southeast Asia:

- **Running K-12 and higher-ed motions with one team.** Different buyers, cycles and proof requirements. Pick one for the launch quarter.
- **Translating the website instead of localising the proof.** A local case study with a local number outperforms a translated homepage every time.
- **Hiring sales before the narrative exists.** Salespeople cannot invent a story; they can only repeat one that works. Write it first.
- **Measuring registrations and downloads.** Those are inputs. Qualified opportunities and pilot-to-paid conversion are what the board should see.
- **Letting every deal be a bespoke negotiation.** Three custom proposals a month is a product and pricing problem, not a sales problem.
- **Entering three markets at once with one marketing hire.** Win one market properly, then copy the motion with the reference account in hand — the same discipline as moving from services into a product, as in [a corporate learning launch](/work/corporate-learning-launch).

## Frequently asked questions

### Which Southeast Asian market should we enter first?

Singapore if you need a credibility reference account and can survive a slow, committee-driven first sale. Indonesia if your product is price-accessible and you can find the right partner. Vietnam if your proof is strong and your category is English-language or STEM. The wrong answer is all three in the same quarter.

### How do we market to schools when teachers do not hold the budget?

Treat teachers and academic leads as champions and arm them for internal selling: one-page outcome summaries, a pilot proposal they can forward without editing, and a named number such as hours of marking saved or pass-rate improvement. Then market to the people who sign — principals, cluster directors, finance leads.

### How long until a launch shows pipeline?

Expect qualified opportunities within four to eight weeks if the positioning work was done, pilot starts within a quarter, and revenue impact across two to three quarters, because K-12 cycles run 9–18 months. Plan the budget for that, not for a same-quarter return.
