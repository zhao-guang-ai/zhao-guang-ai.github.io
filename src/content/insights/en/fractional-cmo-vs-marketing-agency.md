A marketing agency executes a brief. A fractional CMO writes the brief, owns the number behind it, and manages the agencies doing the execution. That one sentence explains most of the confusion — and most of the money wasted by companies that bought the second thing while believing they were buying the first.

The uncomfortable version: if you have changed agencies twice in eighteen months, the problem is almost certainly not the agencies.

## The difference in one paragraph

An agency's unit of work is a deliverable: a campaign, a landing page, a content calendar, an event. A fractional CMO's unit of work is a decision plus the number behind it: who we sell to, what we charge, which two channels get funded, what we stop doing, and what cost per qualified opportunity we are willing to accept for each. Agencies are measured on output and, at best, on activity metrics. A fractional CMO is measured on whether the pipeline moved.

This is why the two are not competitors in a well-run company. They occupy different layers. The agency is the hands; the fractional CMO is the person deciding what the hands should do and checking whether it worked.

## When an agency is the right answer

Buy production capacity when the thinking is already done. That means:

- **You can write the brief yourself in an hour.** If you know the audience, the message, the channel and the success metric without deliberating, you do not need senior strategy. You need throughput.
- **You can judge the output.** You can look at a campaign and say whether it is on-brand, on-message and likely to work. Without that ability, an agency can bill you for twelve months of work you have no way to evaluate.
- **Positioning, ICP and pricing are settled and written down.** Not "we all know it" — written down, in a document a new hire could read.
- **The bottleneck is a specific, bounded gap.** A product launch needing six weeks of campaign production, a rebrand needing design, an event needing execution. Bounded problems suit agencies; open-ended ones do not.

Typical spend for that: S$8,000–25,000 a month on retainer in Singapore, or S$5,000–15,000 for a single campaign project. Perfectly reasonable money — when the brief exists.

## When a fractional CMO is the right answer

Buy decision-making when the thinking is the thing that is missing. The symptoms are consistent:

- **You cannot explain why you win or lose.** Deals close and you are not sure which message did it. That is a positioning gap, and no amount of campaign production closes it.
- **Two channels are being funded on faith.** S$15,000–30,000 a month going out, and nobody can state the cost per qualified opportunity for either channel.
- **Sales and marketing tell different stories.** The website promises one thing, the demo promises another, and the proposal a third. Buyers notice, even if your team does not.
- **Your last three agency engagements produced work nobody could evaluate.** Beautiful decks, activity reports, and no line connecting any of it to revenue.
- **Marketing has become the founder's evening job.** Nobody else has the context to make the call, so the call keeps being deferred to 11pm.

The diagnostic I would apply before spending anything: **if you have replaced your agency twice in eighteen months, stop shopping for a third.** Changing the executor does not fix an unwritten brief. In my experience the third agency produces roughly the same outcome as the first two, at a higher hourly rate.

## The hybrid model, and why it usually wins

The structure that works best for most Series A and B companies is a fractional CMO plus an agency, with a written scope of who owns what:

- **Fractional CMO at six days a month — S$10,000–13,000.** Owns positioning, pricing, the channel plan, the team, hiring and board reporting.
- **Agency at S$10,000–15,000 a month.** Owns production: paid campaigns, content volume, design, event execution.
- **Total: roughly S$250,000–330,000 a year.** Still below the S$380,000–480,000 fully loaded cost of a single full-time CMO, and you get both senior ownership and output.

The reason this beats either option alone is not the money — it is that the agency finally has someone managing it. Most agency waste is not agency incompetence. It is an unsupervised agency optimising for the metrics it can control rather than the ones your business needs.

**On the in-house third option.** A marketing manager at S$90,000–140,000 a year is genuinely cheaper than a fractional CMO, and for some companies it is the right hire. The question to ask is not "can we afford it" but "who manages them?". A first-time marketing manager with no senior guidance will produce activity and learn slowly, and you will spend founder time you do not have. If nobody in the building can judge marketing work, that is the gap to fill before adding headcount — and it is the gap a fractional CMO fills.

## Common mistakes

Six patterns that reliably waste money, in rough order of how often I see them:

- **Hiring an agency to fix a positioning problem.** The agency will produce beautiful work for the wrong audience, on time and on budget, and nothing will change.
- **Asking an agency for strategy — then being surprised by the invoice.** Strategy work is priced as senior time. If it is cheap, it is being done by someone junior with a template.
- **Letting the agency talk directly to the board.** Now you have two competing narratives and no single owner of the number.
- **Briefing by symptom.** "We need more leads" produces more leads. "We need qualified opportunities in school networks at under S$1,500 each" produces a plan you can argue with.
- **No agreed definition of a qualified opportunity.** If sales and marketing define it differently, every pipeline meeting becomes a dispute about whose leads are worse.
- **Paying for activity reports instead of pipeline.** Impressions, clicks and engagement rate are inputs. Cost per qualified opportunity, pilot-to-paid conversion and coverage against target are the outputs. Buy the outputs.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can we have both at the same time?

Yes, and for most companies at Series A and B it is the strongest configuration — provided the split is written down. The failure mode is hiring both without agreeing who owns positioning and who reports to the board. One paragraph of scope prevents it.

### Who manages the agency in that model?

The fractional CMO does: briefs, reviews, budget allocation and holding them to the pipeline number rather than the delivery date. That is one of the clearest differences between the two roles — an agency can be managed by anyone with taste; a fractional CMO is the person who does the managing.

### Isn't a fractional CMO more expensive than an agency?

Per hour, yes. Per useful outcome, usually not. An agency at S$15,000 a month producing campaigns for the wrong segment is more expensive than a fractional CMO at S$12,000 a month who spends the first three weeks killing two channels and rewriting the message. Compare the two on cost per qualified opportunity, not on monthly fee.

### What if we already have a marketing team?

Then the fractional CMO leads them, and you may not need an agency at all — the team becomes the production capacity. Two or three capable marketers plus six days a month of senior direction is one of the most efficient structures I work in.

### How long before we can tell whether it is working?

Tracking and routing fixes show up in four to six weeks. Channel-level cost per qualified opportunity clarity takes about a quarter. The pipeline number follows your sales cycle, which in education is often six to twelve months — which is exactly why the leading indicators matter more than the lagging ones.
