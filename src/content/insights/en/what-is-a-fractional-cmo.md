A fractional CMO is a senior marketing executive who works for you part-time — usually two to three days a week, on a six to twelve month engagement — and owns the marketing function the way a full-time hire would. Strategy, the team, the budget, the board reporting, and the number at the end of it.

That definition matters, because most founders asking this question are not shopping for a job title. They are trying to work out whether the gap in their business is leadership or capacity. Those are two different problems with two very different price tags, and confusing them is the most expensive mistake in this whole area.

## What a fractional CMO actually is

The role is defined by accountability, not by hours. An advisor gives you a recommendation and leaves; a fractional CMO gives you a recommendation, then sits in the room while it gets executed, and is judged on whether it worked. Concretely, that means:

- **They own the marketing number.** Not pipeline "influenced", but the number you agreed on: cost per qualified opportunity, pilot-to-paid conversion, pipeline coverage against target.
- **They lead your people.** Your two or three marketers report into them, or at least take weekly direction from them. This is the part agencies cannot do for you.
- **They make the calls you have been deferring.** Positioning, pricing architecture, which channels to kill, which hire comes next.
- **They are in the room when it counts.** Board meetings, investor updates, the quarterly review where marketing spend gets questioned.

Three roles get confused with it, and the difference is who carries the consequence:

- **A marketing consultant** advises. Somebody inside your company still decides.
- **An interim CMO** covers a gap, usually close to full-time and short-term.
- **A marketing agency** executes a brief. Writing it, and owning the outcome, are not in the contract.

## When a fractional CMO is the right call

Five triggers I see repeatedly, each with a way to test whether it is actually your situation:

- **The founder is still the marketing department.** Writing landing pages at 11pm and approving ad copy between sales calls is not an effort problem — it is that nobody else has the context to decide. Test: count your weekly hours on marketing execution. Above five, you have a leadership gap, not a workload problem.
- **You are spending real money on faith.** S$15,000–30,000 a month across paid, content and events, with no defensible cost per qualified opportunity on any channel. Test: if you cannot name your two best channels and their numbers within thirty seconds, the budget is currently unaudited.
- **You have marketers but no marketing leader.** Two or three capable people who cannot name this quarter's three priorities, because nobody set them. A fourth marketer will not fix that; it makes it more expensive.
- **The board keeps asking for a plan you keep improvising.** If monthly reporting means rebuilding a deck the night before, you have activity, not a marketing function.
- **You are six to twelve months away from justifying a full-time CMO.** A full-time CMO in Singapore is S$380,000–480,000 fully loaded per year, and the search takes four to six months once you start. Most Series A and B companies cannot absorb that yet, but they cannot wait a year for senior direction either.

## When it is the wrong call

I turn down more of these than I take, and the reasons are consistent:

- **You do not yet have product-market fit.** Below roughly S$1M ARR with no repeatable sales motion, the job is customer conversations and product decisions, not a marketing plan. You would spend S$60,000 on a tidy strategy for a product that has not found its buyer.
- **You need capacity, not decisions.** If positioning is settled, the ICP is clear and the bottleneck is production volume, buy an agency or a contractor. Paying strategy rates for execution is the most common way this budget gets wasted.
- **Nobody will actually hand over authority.** If pricing and messaging decisions still route through a founder who overrules them, the engagement fails politely and expensively. Senior people without decision rights are an expensive opinion.
- **You want a cheaper CMO.** This is not a discount version of a full-time hire. It is a different structure: fewer days, sharper scope, and an expectation that your team executes. If your team is one intern and a freelance designer, you are buying strategy with nobody to run it.

## What the first 90 days look like

Any credible fractional CMO should be able to describe this before you sign:

- **Weeks 1–3 — diagnose.** Interviews with your team, your best customers and three churned accounts. An audit of every channel and its real cost. A written answer to "where is growth leaking, and what is it costing us per month", with a number in it.
- **Weeks 3–5 — plan.** One page: ICP, positioning, channel mix, budget allocation, and the two or three metrics that define success. Signed off by the board, not just by you. Fifteen slides means it is not a decision yet.
- **Months 2–4 — build.** Messaging rewritten and tested with real buyers. Priority channels launched. Tracking fixed, so the next performance conversation is about data, not opinions. Hiring scorecards written if you are adding headcount.

If a prospective fractional CMO cannot tell you what they will hand over in week three, they are planning to bill you for figuring it out.

## What it costs, and how the scope is set

In Singapore, a fractional CMO typically costs S$10,000–13,000 a month for six days of work, or S$14,000–18,000 for eight days. That is roughly 30–38% of the fully loaded cost of a full-time CMO, for the same seniority. I have broken the numbers down properly in [what a fractional CMO costs in Singapore](/insights/fractional-cmo-cost-singapore).

Two scoping rules I would hold any provider to. Contract in days per month, not deliverables — deliverables stretch to fill whatever you agreed, and days are auditable. And set a three-month minimum: anything shorter produces an audit, not a change in trajectory.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many days a week does a fractional CMO actually work?

Two to three days a week is the standard rhythm: one recurring leadership meeting, one working session on the current priority, plus asynchronous review. Below four days a month you are buying advice rather than ownership. Above three days a week you are usually better off hiring full-time.

### How is this different from hiring a marketing agency?

An agency executes a brief; a fractional CMO writes the brief, owns the number and manages the agency. If you know exactly what needs producing and can judge the output, buy the agency. If you cannot articulate why you are winning or losing deals, the brief is the missing piece, and more production will not help. I compare the two in [fractional CMO vs marketing agency](/insights/fractional-cmo-vs-marketing-agency).

### Do we need an in-house marketing team already?

No, but you need at least one competent executor — a marketer, a designer, or a reliable agency — for the strategy to land anywhere. The pattern that works best is a fractional CMO at six to eight days a month plus one or two in-house people who do the work and learn the reasoning behind it.

### What happens at the end of the engagement?

The goal is that you no longer need the role part-time: a documented playbook, a team that knows what to do on Monday morning, and either a full-time marketing leader hired with your involvement or a decision that six days a month is still the right structure. I write the job scorecard, sit on the interview panel, and stay for a structured handover.

### Can a fractional CMO be based somewhere else?

Yes, and for Singapore companies working across Indonesia, Vietnam and Malaysia, someone who travels the region is often an advantage. Avoid a time-zone gap of more than four hours from your sales team — the value is in the unplanned conversations.
