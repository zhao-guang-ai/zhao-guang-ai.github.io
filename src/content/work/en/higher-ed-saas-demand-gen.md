A higher-education SaaS company selling a student lifecycle and enrolment CRM to universities in Singapore, Malaysia and Australia. Six account executives, a marketing team of three, healthy-looking lead volume, and a leadership team that had stopped believing any of the numbers. Six months, three days a week.

## The situation

Marketing produced roughly 450 leads a month. About 4% became sales-qualified, and nobody could say which channel any of them came from. Spend was split across seven channels at around S$32,000 a month.

The weekly pipeline meeting had become a ritual argument. Sales said the leads were junk. Marketing said sales never followed up. Both were describing the same underlying problem from different sides: there was no agreed definition of a qualified opportunity, so each side was measuring against its own private standard.

Two consequences followed from that. First, the CRM was full of duplicates and had no lifecycle stages, so forecasting was guesswork — the number moved by roughly ±35% against plan each quarter. Second, pipeline coverage against target sat at 1.8x, which for a nine-month education sales cycle is not a buffer, it is a warning.

## What we did

**Defined a qualified opportunity in writing, with sales, in one meeting.** Four criteria: a named institution, an identified budget owner, a mapped buying-committee role, and a next step with a date. Anything that failed one of the four was a lead, not an opportunity, and was not counted in the pipeline. This was the single highest-leverage change of the engagement, and it cost nothing but an hour of disagreement.

**Instrumented the funnel end to end.** First touch to closed-won in HubSpot, including the offline sources that had been invisible: conferences, partner referrals and the dean who emails an AE directly. You cannot manage a nine-month cycle with last-click attribution.

**Cut seven paid channels to two.** After a 60-day read on cost per qualified opportunity, two channels were defensible and five were not. The five were not obviously broken — they produced traffic and form fills — but the numbers could not justify them once opportunities, rather than leads, were the unit of account.

**Rebuilt lead scoring around buying-committee roles.** Registrar, CIO, provost, finance and faculty champion, scored differently from each other, because a faculty champion and a procurement director require entirely different conversations. Job-title-only scoring had been flattening those differences for two years.

**Built two nurture tracks sized to a nine-month consideration window.** One for institutions in early research, one for institutions with a named project. Most education nurture fails because it is written for a 30-day decision that does not exist.

**Produced sales enablement the committee could use.** A one-pager per committee role, an objection-handling sheet drawn from real lost deals, and three customer case studies with numbers in them.

**Shipped a weekly dashboard that leadership actually read.** Cost per qualified opportunity, coverage ratio against target, and forecast accuracy — three numbers, one page.

## The result

- **Cost per qualified opportunity: down 37%.** Mostly from the channel cut and from disqualifying leads earlier, before they consumed sales time.
- **Qualified opportunity to win rate: 18% → 26%.** Better-qualified opportunities convert better; the two effects compound.
- **Pipeline coverage against target: 1.8x → 3.1x.** In a nine-month cycle this is the number that changes how the board feels about the year.
- **Lead volume: down about 40%, while qualified opportunities rose.** Deliberately. The 450-lead figure had been a vanity metric that made the pipeline meeting worse.
- **Forecast accuracy: roughly ±35% → roughly ±12%.**

The honest part: cutting lead volume was politically difficult for about two months, and one of the channels we killed turned out to have been sourcing about 3% of revenue. We found that out because the tracking finally worked, not because the decision was obviously right at the time.
