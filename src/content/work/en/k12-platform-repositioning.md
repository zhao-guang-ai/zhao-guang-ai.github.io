A Series B K-12 platform with a genuinely good product, forty-plus school logos on the website, and revenue that had been flat for four consecutive quarters. I was brought in for nine months at two days a week to work out why.

## The situation

The numbers were not mysterious, they were just unexamined. Average contract value sat around S$9,000. The sales cycle ran about eleven months. Churn after the first year was high enough that new logos were mostly replacing the revenue the old ones took with them.

Marketing was spending roughly S$18,000 a month across seven channels with no attribution worth the name. Nobody could say which channel produced a qualified opportunity, because the company had never defined what a qualified opportunity was. The founder was still writing most of the copy, usually late at night, and the sales team was working from a deck he had built two years earlier.

The product was being sold school by school, most often to a teacher or a head of department who loved it. That is a warm way to start and a terrible way to scale: individual schools had small budgets, needed as much hand-holding as a ten-school rollout, and renewed on a teacher's enthusiasm rather than an institutional commitment.

## What we did

**Repositioned from schools to networks.** The value of the product — consistency of assessment data across a group of schools — only appeared at the network level, and so did the budget. We stopped treating a private chain of twenty schools as twenty deals and started treating it as one buyer with a rollout plan.

**Built a network tier.** A single package priced at S$45,000–80,000 a year, with a defined rollout sequence across schools, an implementation plan and a review cadence. Same product, different scope, and an ACV roughly five times the old one.

**Rewrote the message around two measurable promises.** Learning gains visible within one term, and hours of teacher marking time saved per week. Both were demonstrable from existing usage data — they had simply never been the headline. The old messaging led with features, which is why the product kept being bought as a nice-to-have.

**Cut seven channels to two.** A partner and referral motion into school networks, plus a content and events motion timed to the school-year budget cycle. The five channels we stopped were not all bad; they were unmeasured, and running five unmeasured channels is not diversification, it is noise.

**Rebuilt tracking before spending another dollar.** Cost per qualified opportunity and pilot-to-paid conversion became visible weekly, which meant every later decision had something to argue with.

**Made pilots end in a decision.** A pilot agreement template with written success criteria, a named executive sponsor, a fixed ten-week window, and the review meeting booked in the calendar before kick-off.

**Coached the founder out of the sales narrative.** Two AEs learned to run the network story themselves, which is the only way the motion survives after an engagement ends.

## The result

- **Cost per qualified opportunity: S$2,100 → S$1,400, a 34% reduction.** Most of it came from the channel cut and from the clearer definition of what counted as qualified.
- **Pilot-to-paid conversion: 19% → 38%.** The pilot agreement did most of this work. Pilots stopped being an open-ended courtesy and became an eight-to-ten week evaluation with agreed criteria.
- **Average contract value: +42%.** Driven by the network tier, not by raising the per-school price.
- **Sales cycle: about 11 months → about 7.5 months.** Fewer stakeholders per deal, and a buying process the buyer had already been walked through once.

The honest part: in the first quarter after repositioning, they lost a number of small single-school deals, and two people on the team argued hard against walking away from that revenue. It was the right trade — those accounts consumed disproportionate support time and almost never expanded — but it did not feel like progress at the time, and I would schedule that conversation earlier next time.
