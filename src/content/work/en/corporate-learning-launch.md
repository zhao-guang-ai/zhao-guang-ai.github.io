A thirty-person corporate training business in Singapore with about S$4M in annual services revenue. They had spent fourteen months and a serious amount of cash building a self-paced SaaS version of their best-selling compliance programme. The product was good. The launch was not. An eight-week go-to-market sprint, then a quarter of hands-on support.

## The situation

Two quarters after launch they had four signed contracts and a sales cycle of about 5.2 months. Four problems, all of them fixable and none of them about the product.

**Pricing had been guessed from the services price list.** A S$39 per-seat figure carried over from instructor-led delivery. It produced no margin at SaaS volumes and, worse, it made enterprise buyers nervous — a price that low signals a product that will not be supported in three years.

**Sales was calling the wrong person with the wrong deck.** The team was pitching HR generalists using material written for L&D directors. Neither audience was the economic buyer.

**There was no pilot-to-contract path.** Every deal became a bespoke negotiation, mostly about legal terms, because there was no standard way to start small and expand.

**Marketing had produced a launch webinar and a brochure.** Both fine, neither connected to how a compliance product actually gets bought — which is against a deadline, by someone whose job is at risk if the deadline is missed.

## What we did

**Segment prioritisation into regulated industries first.** Banking, healthcare and logistics, where compliance training is mandatory and the buying trigger is a regulatory deadline rather than a preference. This was the decision that made everything else work: selling against a deadline compresses a sales cycle in a way no amount of marketing polish can.

**Repackaged into three tiers, with the enterprise tier anchored at S$28,000 a year for up to 500 seats.** That anchor made the per-seat maths defensible, gave sales something to trade down from instead of discounting from the start, and let the entry tier stay deliberately small.

**Mapped the buying committee properly.** CHRO as sponsor, L&D director as champion, compliance officer as the risk owner who can block, IT security as the technical blocker. Then rebuilt the deck per role, because a compliance officer and a CHRO are buying different things: one buys audit evidence, the other buys coverage across the group.

**Created a 90-day paid pilot agreement with written success criteria.** Completion rate above 80%, pass rate above 85%, and a manager-reported time saving. A named executive sponsor, and the review date booked before kick-off. Paid, because a free pilot is a favour and a favour does not get reviewed.

**Built the sales narrative with the founder rather than for him.** He knew the compliance problem better than anyone in the market and could not explain it in a way a buyer could repeat. We wrote the version he could actually say out loud.

**Produced one-pagers per committee role and three proof pieces** drawn from the services business's existing clients — real logos, real completion numbers, no new logos invented.

**Ran two channels: LinkedIn with a compliance-deadline hook, and partner referrals** through the two audit firms the services business already worked with. Nobody in the target segment was searching for a new compliance platform, so demand had to be created against a date they already feared.

## The result

- **14 signed contracts in the two quarters after the sprint**, against 4 in the two quarters before it.
- **Average contract value: S$28,000**, with the first three deals all landing on the enterprise tier.
- **Pilot-to-paid conversion: 62%.** Well above the 30–50% that counts as good, and the reason is the segmentation: these pilots were sold into regulated buyers with hard deadlines, not into willing experimentalists.
- **Sales cycle: 5.2 → 3.4 months.**
- **Cost per qualified opportunity: about S$1,900 → about S$1,250.**

The honest part: the first pricing attempt was rejected by three of their own existing services clients in a single week, which is what forced the tiering work. Being told by your own customers that the price is wrong is uncomfortable and considerably cheaper than finding out over two quarters of discounting.
